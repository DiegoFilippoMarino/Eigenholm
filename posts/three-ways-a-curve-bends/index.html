Three Ways a Curve Bends

Generalization
Scaling Laws
Mechanistic Interpretability
Double descent, emergent abilities, and grokking are three different kinds of sharpness, and a short derivation shows that conjunctive metrics move a threshold without making it steeper.
Author

Diego Filippo Marino

Published

August 14, 2026

Three results in deep learning are routinely filed under the same heading. Test risk rises and then falls again as models grow past the interpolation threshold (Belkin et al. 2019; Nakkiran et al. 2020). Downstream capabilities appear abruptly above a scale that smooth loss curves did not predict (Wei et al. 2022). Networks memorize an algorithmic task, sit at chance validation accuracy for orders of magnitude more optimization, and then generalize almost at once (Power et al. 2022).

Calling all three a phase transition is not wrong, but it flattens a distinction that matters for anyone who has to forecast. In one of them the risk itself is genuinely non-monotone. In another the underlying quantity is smooth and the reported number is a transform of it. In the third the mechanism forms gradually and the reported number is the last step of a process that started much earlier.

This article separates them by building each one small enough to inspect: an exact double-descent curve from random features, a closed-form analysis of what conjunctive metrics do to a smooth power law, and a grokking run with mechanistic progress measures that fits in a few dozen seconds of NumPy. The middle one produces a result I did not expect, which is that the standard argument about metrics is half wrong in an instructive way.

Bend one: the risk is really non-monotone

Start with the case where nothing is hiding. Fit a linear model on p random features to n training points by minimum-norm least squares, and sweep p through n.

Let \Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} be the feature matrix and y the targets. For p < n the ordinary least-squares solution is \hat\beta = \Phi^{+} y, and for p > n the same pseudoinverse returns the interpolating solution of smallest norm, that is, the solution of

\min_{\beta} \lVert \beta \rVert_2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Phi\beta = y . \tag{1}

The interesting quantity is the smallest nonzero singular value of \Phi. Below the threshold, adding features improves the fit. Exactly at p = n the matrix is square and, for random features, nearly singular: the smallest singular value concentrates near zero, so \hat\beta = \Phi^{-1}y has enormous norm and the noise in y is amplified without bound. Above the threshold the constraint set in Equation 1 becomes an affine subspace of growing dimension, the minimum-norm point moves away from the ill-conditioned corner, and the solution becomes smooth again.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is visible in one plot.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

n_train, n_test, d_input = 80, 2000, 12
noise_sd = 0.35
widths = np.unique(np.round(np.geomspace(2, 900, 46)).astype(int))


def experiment(width, rng):
    W = rng.normal(size=(d_input, width)) / np.sqrt(d_input)
    beta_star = rng.normal(size=d_input) / np.sqrt(d_input)

    def features(X):
        return np.maximum(X @ W, 0.0)

    X_tr = rng.normal(size=(n_train, d_input))
    X_te = rng.normal(size=(n_test, d_input))
    y_tr = X_tr @ beta_star + noise_sd * rng.normal(size=n_train)
    y_te = X_te @ beta_star

    Phi_tr, Phi_te = features(X_tr), features(X_te)
    beta = np.linalg.pinv(Phi_tr, rcond=1e-12) @ y_tr
    risk = float(np.mean((Phi_te @ beta - y_te) ** 2))
    smallest = float(np.linalg.svd(Phi_tr, compute_uv=False)[min(width, n_train) - 1])
    return risk, float(np.linalg.norm(beta)), smallest


trials = 25
risks = np.zeros(len(widths))
norms = np.zeros(len(widths))
sigmas = np.zeros(len(widths))
for k, width in enumerate(widths):
    for t in range(trials):
        r, nb, s = experiment(width, np.random.default_rng(1000 * t + width))
        risks[k] += r / trials
        norms[k] += nb / trials
        sigmas[k] += s / trials

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.6, 3.9))
axes[0].loglog(widths / n_train, risks, "o-", ms=4, color="#2a6e6e")
axes[0].axvline(1.0, color="black", ls=":", lw=1.1)
axes[0].set(xlabel="features / training points", ylabel="test risk",
            title="Model-wise double descent")

axes[1].loglog(widths / n_train, norms, "o-", ms=4, color="#a4553a", label=r"$\Vert\hat\beta\Vert_2$")
axes[1].loglog(widths / n_train, sigmas, "s-", ms=4, color="#4c6ea8",
               label="smallest singular value")
axes[1].axvline(1.0, color="black", ls=":", lw=1.1)
axes[1].set(xlabel="features / training points", title="What happens at the threshold")
axes[1].legend(frameon=False)
for ax in axes:
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The peak in the left panel sits at a ratio of one, and the right panel shows why: the smallest singular value dips by orders of magnitude there, and the solution norm mirrors it. No metric transformation is involved, no thresholding, no discontinuous scoring rule. The expected squared error, the smoothest metric available, is genuinely non-monotone in model size.

This is worth emphasizing because it rules out the explanation that gets applied to the other two cases. Whatever double descent is, it is not an artifact of how we scored the model. Nakkiran et al. make the same phenomenon appear along the training-time and sample-size axes by defining effective model complexity, the largest training set a procedure can fit to near-zero error, and observing that the peak tracks the point where that quantity equals n regardless of which axis moved it (Nakkiran et al. 2020). Label noise sharpens the peak because noise is precisely what an ill-conditioned interpolant amplifies.

Bend two: what a conjunctive metric actually does

Now the case where a transformation is involved. The standard argument runs: cross-entropy falls smoothly with scale, but a benchmark scored by exact match requires every token to be right, so a smooth per-token improvement becomes a sharp jump in the reported number. Emergence is then partly an artifact of choosing a discontinuous metric.

The first half of this is right and the second half deserves a calculation.

Let L(N) be the per-token excess loss at scale N, following the usual power law L(N) = cN^{-\alpha}. Take a task whose answer is k tokens long and suppose token errors are roughly independent, so the probability of getting the whole answer right is

\mathrm{EM}(N) \;=\; \exp\bigl(-k\,L(N)\bigr) \;=\; \exp\bigl(-k\,c\,N^{-\alpha}\bigr). \tag{2}

Write u = kcN^{-\alpha}, so \mathrm{EM} = e^{-u} and u \propto N^{-\alpha}. Two quantities follow immediately.

The threshold, defined as the scale at which the metric crosses one half, is where u = \ln 2:

N_{50} = \left(\frac{k\,c}{\ln 2}\right)^{1/\alpha}. \tag{3}

The width, defined as the number of decades between the 10% and 90% crossings, comes from u = \ln 10 and u = \ln(10/9):

\log_{10}\frac{N_{90}}{N_{10}} = \frac{1}{\alpha}\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\ln 10}{\ln (10/9)}\right) \approx \frac{1.34}{\alpha}. \tag{4}

The width does not contain k. Requiring a hundred tokens instead of one moves the threshold by \log_{10}(100)/\alpha decades and leaves the shape of the transition exactly as it was. On a log-scale horizontal axis, the curves for different task lengths are translates of one another.

So the conjunction is not what makes the curve sharp. The scaling exponent is. A capability whose underlying loss improves with \alpha = 1 transitions over about 1.3 decades of scale, which looks abrupt on a plot spanning eight decades. A capability with \alpha = 0.1 transitions over thirteen decades, which looks like nothing at all. Conjunction sets when, the exponent sets how fast.

scales = np.geomspace(1e2, 1e14, 800)
c_const = 0.5


def exact_match(scale, k, alpha):
    return np.exp(-k * c_const * scale ** (-alpha))


fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.8, 3.9))

alpha = 0.35
for k, color in zip([1, 20, 400], ["#2a6e6e", "#4c6ea8", "#a4553a"]):
    axes[0].semilogx(scales, exact_match(scales, k, alpha), lw=1.8, color=color,
                     label=f"answer length {k}")
    predicted = (k * c_const / np.log(2.0)) ** (1.0 / alpha)
    axes[0].plot([predicted], [0.5], "o", ms=6, mfc="white", color=color)
axes[0].axhline(0.5, color="black", ls=":", lw=0.9)
axes[0].set(xlabel="scale $N$", ylabel="exact-match accuracy",
            title=fr"Answer length shifts, $\alpha={alpha}$")
axes[0].legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9)

for a, color in zip([0.15, 0.35, 0.9], ["#a4553a", "#4c6ea8", "#2a6e6e"]):
    curve = exact_match(scales, 20, a)
    axes[1].semilogx(scales / (20 * c_const / np.log(2.0)) ** (1.0 / a), curve, lw=1.8,
                     color=color, label=fr"$\alpha={a}$, width $\approx${1.34 / a:.1f} decades")
axes[1].axhline(0.5, color="black", ls=":", lw=0.9)
axes[1].set(xlabel="scale, in units of the crossing point", ylabel="exact-match accuracy",
            title="The exponent sets the width")
axes[1].legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9)
for ax in axes:
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

for k in (1, 20, 400):
    lo = (k * c_const / np.log(10.0)) ** (1 / alpha)
    hi = (k * c_const / np.log(10.0 / 9.0)) ** (1 / alpha)
    print(f"k = {k:3d}   crossing at N = {(k * c_const / np.log(2)) ** (1 / alpha):.3g}"
          f"   width = {np.log10(hi / lo):.2f} decades")
k =   1   crossing at N = 0.393   width = 3.83 decades
k =  20   crossing at N = 2.05e+03   width = 3.83 decades
k = 400   crossing at N = 1.07e+07   width = 3.83 decades

The printed widths are identical to two decimal places across three orders of magnitude in task length, exactly as Equation 4 requires.

This changes what the metric argument is claiming. It is not that scoring by exact match manufactures sharpness out of smoothness. It is that scoring by exact match, or by any conjunction over a growing number of independent chances, moves the crossing to a scale that depends on the task and is therefore not shared with the aggregate loss curve. Unpredictability of emergence, in this model, is unpredictability of location, not of shape.

Two things do genuinely manufacture sharpness. A metric with an actual discontinuity, such as scoring a task as solved only when the error rate falls below a fixed tolerance, produces a step function of whatever width the noise gives it. And a break in the underlying law produces a break in everything downstream of it. The broken neural scaling law is the functional form built for the second case, a base power law multiplied by smooth transition factors, deliberately able to express both non-monotone dips and sharp inflections that a single power law cannot (Caballero et al. 2023). Reading Equation 3 alongside that form, an honest summary is that some apparent emergence is a relocation of a smooth curve, some is a real break in the law, and telling them apart requires knowing the exponent, not just looking at the plot.

Bend three: the metric is late, not wrong

The third case is different from both. Here the reported metric is genuinely flat for a long time and then genuinely jumps, and the underlying mechanism is neither smooth-and-relocated nor discontinuous. It is a structure that assembles gradually and only becomes visible when a competing structure is removed.

The setup is modular addition, the standard grokking testbed. The model below is a two-layer network rather than the one-layer transformer used in the mechanistic study, which makes the point that the phenomenon is about the optimization, not the architecture: shared embeddings for both operands, one hidden layer, cross-entropy, full-batch AdamW with decoupled weight decay, and half of the multiplication table held out.

MODULUS, EMBED, HIDDEN = 31, 32, 128


def make_split(rng, train_frac=0.5):
    a, b = np.meshgrid(np.arange(MODULUS), np.arange(MODULUS), indexing="ij")
    a, b, y = a.ravel(), b.ravel(), (a.ravel() + b.ravel()) % MODULUS
    order = rng.permutation(len(y))
    cut = int(train_frac * len(y))
    tr, te = order[:cut], order[cut:]
    return (a[tr], b[tr], y[tr]), (a[te], b[te], y[te])


def init_params(rng):
    return {
        "E": rng.normal(size=(MODULUS, EMBED)) / np.sqrt(EMBED),
        "W1": rng.normal(size=(2 * EMBED, HIDDEN)) / np.sqrt(2 * EMBED),
        "W2": rng.normal(size=(HIDDEN, MODULUS)) / np.sqrt(HIDDEN),
    }


def forward(par, a, b):
    x = np.concatenate([par["E"][a], par["E"][b]], axis=1)
    pre = x @ par["W1"]
    hid = np.maximum(pre, 0.0)
    return x, pre, hid, hid @ par["W2"]


def softmax_stats(logits, y):
    logits = logits - logits.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    probs = np.exp(logits)
    probs /= probs.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    loss = -np.mean(np.log(probs[np.arange(len(y)), y] + 1e-12))
    acc = float(np.mean(np.argmax(probs, axis=1) == y))
    return probs, loss, acc


def evaluate(par, a, b, y):
    _, _, _, logits = forward(par, a, b)
    _, loss, acc = softmax_stats(logits, y)
    return loss, acc


def gradients(par, a, b, y):
    x, pre, hid, logits = forward(par, a, b)
    probs, loss, acc = softmax_stats(logits, y)
    dlogits = probs
    dlogits[np.arange(len(y)), y] -= 1.0
    dlogits /= len(y)
    grads = {"W2": hid.T @ dlogits}
    dpre = (dlogits @ par["W2"].T) * (pre > 0)
    grads["W1"] = x.T @ dpre
    dx = dpre @ par["W1"].T
    gE = np.zeros_like(par["E"])
    np.add.at(gE, a, dx[:, :EMBED])
    np.add.at(gE, b, dx[:, EMBED:])
    grads["E"] = gE
    return loss, acc, grads


def train(steps=40000, lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1.0, snapshot_every=400, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    (ta, tb, ty), (va, vb, vy) = make_split(rng)
    par = init_params(rng)
    m = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in par.items()}
    v = {k: np.zeros_like(w) for k, w in par.items()}
    snaps = []
    for step in range(1, steps + 1):
        _, _, g = gradients(par, ta, tb, ty)
        for k in par:
            m[k] = 0.9 * m[k] + 0.1 * g[k]
            v[k] = 0.98 * v[k] + 0.02 * g[k] ** 2
            update = (m[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** step)) / (np.sqrt(v[k] / (1 - 0.98 ** step)) + 1e-8)
            par[k] -= lr * (update + weight_decay * par[k])
        if step % snapshot_every == 0:
            snaps.append((step, {k: w.copy() for k, w in par.items()}))
    return snaps, (ta, tb, ty), (va, vb, vy)

Weight decay is not an incidental hyperparameter here. Removing it removes the phenomenon: the network stays at its memorizing solution indefinitely. The decay is what eventually makes the memorized table more expensive than the algorithm.

To see the mechanism rather than the symptom, we need the two measures that the mechanistic study derived from its reverse-engineered circuit (Nanda et al. 2023). The circuit for modular addition works in the Fourier basis, embedding each input as sines and cosines at a small set of key frequencies and composing rotations through angle-addition identities. That suggests reading the embedding matrix in the frequency domain and asking two questions during training. First, if we keep only the key frequencies and delete everything else, how well does the model generalize? Second, if we delete only the key frequencies and keep everything else, how well does the model still fit its training data?

snaps, (ta, tb, ty), (va, vb, vy) = train()

final_E = snaps[-1][1]["E"]
spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(final_E - final_E.mean(axis=0), axis=0)) ** 2
key_freqs = np.argsort(spectrum[1:].sum(axis=1))[::-1][:5] + 1


def keep_frequencies(E, freqs):
    F = np.fft.rfft(E, axis=0)
    G = np.zeros_like(F)
    G[0] = F[0]
    G[freqs] = F[freqs]
    return np.fft.irfft(G, n=E.shape[0], axis=0)


rows = []
for step, par in snaps:
    tr_loss, tr_acc = evaluate(par, ta, tb, ty)
    te_loss, te_acc = evaluate(par, va, vb, vy)
    restricted = {**par, "E": keep_frequencies(par["E"], key_freqs)}
    excluded = {**par, "E": par["E"] - keep_frequencies(par["E"], key_freqs)
                + par["E"].mean(axis=0)}
    _, res_acc = evaluate(restricted, va, vb, vy)
    exc_loss, _ = evaluate(excluded, ta, tb, ty)
    norm = sum(float(np.sum(w ** 2)) for w in par.values())
    rows.append((step, tr_acc, te_acc, res_acc, exc_loss, norm))

hist = np.array(rows)
step, tr_acc, te_acc, res_acc, exc_loss, norm = hist.T

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12.2, 3.7))
axes[0].semilogx(step, tr_acc, lw=1.8, color="#a4553a", label="train")
axes[0].semilogx(step, te_acc, lw=1.8, color="#2a6e6e", label="validation")
axes[0].set(xlabel="optimization step", ylabel="accuracy", title="The visible curve")
axes[0].legend(frameon=False)

axes[1].semilogx(step, te_acc, lw=1.8, color="#2a6e6e", label="validation accuracy")
axes[1].semilogx(step, res_acc, lw=1.8, ls="--", color="#4c6ea8",
                 label=f"restricted to {len(key_freqs)} frequencies")
axes[1].set(xlabel="optimization step", title="The circuit is early")
axes[1].legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9)

axes[2].semilogx(step, exc_loss, lw=1.8, color="#4c6ea8", label="excluded train loss")
axes[2].semilogx(step, norm / norm.max(), lw=1.8, color="#8d8d96",
                 label="weight norm, normalized")
axes[2].set(xlabel="optimization step", title="The memorized solution is removed")
axes[2].legend(frameon=False, fontsize=9)
for ax in axes:
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

fit = step[np.argmax(tr_acc > 0.99)]
grok = step[np.argmax(te_acc > 0.9)]
print(f"train accuracy passes 99% at step {int(fit)}")
print(f"validation accuracy passes 90% at step {int(grok)}, a factor of {grok / fit:.0f} later")
print(f"dominant embedding frequencies: {sorted(key_freqs.tolist())}")
train accuracy passes 99% at step 400
validation accuracy passes 90% at step 29200, a factor of 73 later
dominant embedding frequencies: [10, 12, 13, 14, 15]

The left panel is the phenomenon as usually reported: a factor of roughly seventy between fitting the training set and generalizing, with validation accuracy pinned at chance for most of that interval.

The middle panel is the correction. Deleting all but five embedding frequencies makes the model generalize substantially better than the model it was deleted from, and it does so thousands of steps before the visible curve moves. At the point where validation accuracy is still around one percent, the restricted model is already well above chance. The structure that will eventually generalize is present and functional. It is being drowned out.

The right panel says what does the drowning and what removes it. The excluded model, which keeps everything except the key frequencies, fits the training set well early on, because memorization lives in the discarded components. Then its training loss starts to climb: the network is moving its computation onto the frequency components we deleted. Total weight norm falls throughout, which is the weight decay quietly making the expensive memorized solution unaffordable.

So the sharpness here is real in the metric and misleading about the process. The generalizing circuit is not built at the moment of the jump. It is built earlier, and the jump is the moment the memorizing circuit finishes being deleted.

What the three cases share and what they do not

The unifying methodological point is not that sharpness is always an illusion. It is that “sharp” is a statement about a particular pairing of an axis, a metric, and a mechanism, and the three can be varied independently.

Two limits of what is above. The independence assumption behind Equation 2 is generous: token errors in a real generation are strongly correlated, so a single mistake often derails the rest, which changes the effective k without changing the structure of the argument. And the grokking experiment uses a task where the target algorithm is known, which is exactly what makes the progress measures constructible. The open problem the mechanistic work states plainly is that these measures are hand-derived per task, and they announce that a transition is coming without saying when. Nothing above changes that. What the width formula Equation 4 adds is a small piece of the forecasting problem: given a scaling exponent, the transition has a known duration, so a capability that is going to arrive will not arrive instantaneously, and the useful thing to estimate is where its crossing sits rather than how steep it will be.
